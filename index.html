The one idea

Complementary strands find each other.

Heat separates the two strands of DNA; cooling lets each strand find its partner again. Nearly every test in this primer is an engineered version of that trick: offer the patient’s DNA a short known sequence — a primer or a probe — and see whether it finds a match. Slide the temperature on the model to watch it happen.

What a molecular test can answer

  • What is it? A fusion or mutation can define a disease.
  • How will it behave? Prognosis.
  • What will work? A biomarker that predicts response to a drug.
  • How much is left? Monitoring after treatment.
  • Who else is at risk? Inherited variants and the family.

Read the language: genes, variants and their names.

A gene is a recipe written in four letters. Molecular pathology looks for the misprints that matter, and gives each one a precise name.

exon 1exon 2exon 3

transcription, then splicing removes the introns

mRNAthe working copy
123AAAA

translation: three bases (one codon) per amino acid

Proteindoes the work

Six ways a gene breaks

Name the kind of alteration first. Each one needs a test that was built to see it — sequencing does not see everything.

Germline or somatic?

Germline variants are present in every cell from conception and can be passed on. Test blood or saliva. Somatic variants are acquired in one tissue — the tumor — and are not inherited. Test the tumor.

The same gene can be either: BRCA1 altered in every cell is an inherited risk; altered only in the tumor it is a treatment clue.

Accelerator or brake?

Oncogenes are accelerators: one activating hit is enough (KRAS, EGFR, BRAF, ERBB2, most fusions). Tumor suppressors are brakes: both copies must fail (TP53, RB1, APC, BRCA1/2).

That is why inheriting one faulty brake predisposes to cancer — only one more hit is needed.

02
Stop 2 · about 8 min · Objective 2

Protect the specimen: the test is only as good as the tissue.

Most molecular failures happen before the specimen ever reaches the molecular laboratory. The pathologist’s contribution starts at the bench: the right tube, the right fixative, and enough tumor.

Tube

EDTA for PCR. Heparin for karyotype.

Heparin inhibits PCR, so blood and marrow for PCR or sequencing go in EDTA (lavender top). Karyotyping is the opposite: it needs living cells in sodium heparin (green top), kept at room temperature and never frozen.

Fixative

Neutral buffered formalin, 6 to 72 hours.

Formalin crosslinks and fragments nucleic acid — still usable, and the standard. Heavy-metal and strongly acidic fixatives (B5, Zenker, Bouin) leave DNA that cannot be tested.

Decalcification

Strong acid destroys DNA.

For a bone lesion, hold back a piece that is not decalcified, ask for EDTA decalcification, or send the aspirate or clot section instead.

03
Stop 3 · about 10 min · Objective 3

Amplify: PCR finds one sequence and copies it a billion times.

The polymerase chain reaction is the workhorse. Two short primers bracket the target, a heat-stable polymerase copies what lies between them, and every cycle doubles the product.

Starting from RNA: reverse transcription

RNA viruses, fusion transcripts and gene expression are measured from RNA. Reverse transcriptase first copies RNA into complementary DNA, then PCR proceeds as usual: RT-PCR.

Do not confuseRT-PCR means reverse-transcription PCR. Real-time PCR is the quantitative kind. Doing both is RT-qPCR.

Counting while copying: real-time PCR

Fluorescence is read at every cycle. The cycle at which the signal crosses a fixed threshold is the cycle threshold, Ct. Start with more target and the threshold is reached sooner.

This is how a viral load, or the amount of BCR::ABL1 transcript, becomes a number.

Stop 4 · about 10 min · Objective 4

Sequence: reading the letters, in one gene or in thousands.

Sequencing answers “what exactly is the change?”. Sanger sequencing reads one fragment with great accuracy. Next-generation sequencing reads millions of fragments at once — and counts them.

One target

Sanger sequencing

Readsone fragment, 500–800 bases
Best fora known single-gene question
Sees a variant whenit is in ≥ 15–20% of the DNA
Turnarounddays
Many targets

Next-generation sequencing (NGS)

Readsmillions of short fragments in parallel
Best forpanels of genes, the exome, the genome
Sees a variant whenit is in about 2–5% of the DNA
Turnaroundone to three weeks
  1. Library. Fragment the DNA; add adapters and a barcode for each patient.
  2. Enrich. Capture or amplify only the genes of interest.
  3. Sequence. Read millions of fragments at the same time.
  4. Analyze. Align reads to the reference genome; call and interpret variants.

05
Stop 5 · about 10 min · Objective 5

See chromosomes, and choose the test that can see the lesion.

Some alterations are too large for sequencing to notice and too small for a microscope. Three tools cover the range. The skill is matching the tool to the size and kind of change.

The whole genome, coarsely

Karyotype

Every chromosome at once, at a resolution of about 5–10 million bases. Needs dividing cells, so it takes days to weeks. Sees balanced translocations and gains or losses of whole chromosomes.

One question, fast

FISH

Fluorescence in situ hybridization: a labelled probe lights up one chosen locus. Works on non-dividing cells and paraffin sections in a day or two. It only sees what you ask about.

Copy number, genome-wide

Chromosomal microarray

Gains and losses across the genome down to tens of thousands of bases. First-line for developmental delay and congenital anomalies. Blind to balanced rearrangements.

06
Stop 6 · about 10 min · Objective 6

Interpret the report: a variant is data, the classification is the result.

Finding a variant is the easy part. Deciding what it means — and stating plainly what the test could not see — is what the molecular pathologist actually delivers.

Inherited (germline) variants

Five tiers

PathogenicLikely pathogenicUncertain significanceLikely benignBenign

Act on pathogenic and likely pathogenic. The middle tier is the variant of uncertain significance, VUS.

Tumor (somatic) variants

Four tiers

Tier I · strongTier II · potentialTier III · unknownTier IV · benign

Tier I guides an approved therapy, a diagnosis or a prognosis in this tumor type. Tier III is the somatic equivalent of a VUS.

Pitfall · the VUSA variant of uncertain significance is not a diagnosis. Do not change management on it, and do not test relatives to predict their risk with it. When a VUS is reclassified it is usually downgraded to benign — but most stay uncertain for years.

Why it matters · “negative” has edgesA negative result means nothing was found within the genes covered, among the kinds of alteration the test can detect, above its limit of detection. A sample with too little tumor can be falsely negative.

Go deeper on your own · how a variant is judged, liquid biopsy caveats

What counts as evidence Next level

Is it common in healthy people?

Population databases such as gnomAD. A variant seen frequently in the general population is very unlikely to cause a rare disease.

Has it been seen in disease?

ClinVar collects classifications of inherited variants; COSMIC catalogues variants found in tumors.

What does it do to the protein?

A stop or frameshift in a gene where loss of function causes disease carries weight. Computer predictions for missense changes are weak evidence on their own.

Does it travel with the disease?

Segregation in affected relatives, or a new (de novo) variant in an affected child of unaffected parents.

Preview · liquid biopsyTumor DNA in plasma can be scarce, so a negative plasma result does not exclude a mutation — tissue remains the back-up. And ageing blood cells acquire their own mutations (clonal hematopoiesis), which a plasma test can mistake for tumor variants.

Molecular in cancer: nine results that change what happens next.

In oncology a molecular result does one of three things: it names the disease, predicts its behavior, or picks the drug. These nine are worth recognising on sight.

Following BCR::ABL1 down Next level

Clinical patternTranscript levels are reported on an International Scale so results compare between laboratories. Each ten-fold fall is one “log”. A major molecular response is 0.1% or less — a 3-log reduction.

What to do nextA confirmed rise in a patient who was responding prompts two questions: is the drug being taken, and has the kinase acquired a resistance mutation such as T315I?

Clonality testingEvery lymphocyte rearranges its antigen-receptor genes differently. PCR of those genes gives a broad spread of sizes in a reactive process and a single dominant peak in a lymphoma. A clonal peak supports, but never by itself makes, a diagnosis of malignancy.

Stop 8 · about 7 min · Objective 8

Beyond cancer: inheritance, drug response and infection.

The same tools answer three other everyday questions. Is this condition inherited? How will this patient handle this drug? What organism is this — and how much of it is there?

Inherited disease

Know why you are testing.

  • Diagnostic — a symptomatic patient (cystic fibrosis, fragile X).
  • Predictive — a healthy relative (Huntington disease, BRCA1/2, Lynch syndrome).
  • Carrier — before or during pregnancy (cystic fibrosis, sickle cell).
  • Prenatal and newborn screening.
Drug response

Three enzymes to recognise.

  • CYP2D6 — codeine is a pro-drug. Poor metabolizers get little relief; ultra-rapid metabolizers risk toxicity.
  • CYP2C19 — activates clopidogrel. Poor metabolizers are under-protected.
  • CYP2C9 — clears warfarin. Slow variants need a lower dose.
Infection

Four jobs.

  • Detect — is it there? C. difficile, MRSA, respiratory viruses.
  • Quantify — viral load in HIV, hepatitis B and C.
  • Type — high-risk HPV genotypes.
  • Resist — resistance genes and mutations: mecA, HIV genotype.

Pitfall · detected is not the same as diseaseA positive nucleic-acid test shows that nucleic acid is present — not that the organism is alive or responsible. Test the right patient (a formed stool should not be tested for C. difficile) and do not use PCR as an early test of cure.

The human side

A genetic result belongs to a family, not only to a patient. Predictive testing follows informed consent and counseling. Broad tests can turn up unexpected findings. In the United States, HIPAA protects the privacy of the result and GINA bars genetic discrimination in health insurance and employment — but not in life, disability or long-term-care insurance.
